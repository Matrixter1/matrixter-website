The Power of Awareness

You send a friend a long message about something that matters to you. An hour later, two words arrive. “All right.” By the time you put the phone down, you have decided your friend is cross. You start drafting an equally short reply.

Look again at what happened. A friend sent two words. Who supplied the tone of voice?

Hearing the voice you added

Perhaps you heard yesterday’s argument in those words. Perhaps you were tired of being the one who always reaches out. Those experiences deserve attention. They can also colour an exchange in ways the other person has no way of knowing.

Awareness begins here, in the small distinction between what happened and what you made of it. You can respect a feeling and still ask where its story came from. “I felt dismissed when I read that” leaves room for two people to speak. “You wanted to dismiss me” has already written the other person’s lines.

Your first reading may turn out to be accurate. Asking gives you a chance to find out. It also gives you a clearer basis for deciding what to do, especially when a pattern genuinely needs addressing.

Choosing what deserves your attention

Try bringing this distinction to an ordinary decision. Suppose a colleague asks whether you can take on one more task. You hear yourself say yes. A moment later you resent the work. What did you notice in the instant before you agreed? You might remember a tight jaw, a glance at the clock, or the thought that a good colleague always makes time.

Now you have something specific to work with. Next time, “Let me check what I’ve promised already” may be an honest first answer. This kind of attention makes responsibility practical. You can decide what you are able to offer, then give it with fewer hidden expectations.

A question to take with you

Tonight, choose one moment you keep replaying. Write three short lines. What could someone else in the room have seen or heard? What meaning did you give it? What would you like to ask or do now?

You may end up keeping your original view. You may discover that an apology, a boundary, a phone call or a night’s sleep would serve you better than another hour spent replaying the scene. Either way, you have taken part in shaping what happens next. Even a two-word message can open that possibility.
